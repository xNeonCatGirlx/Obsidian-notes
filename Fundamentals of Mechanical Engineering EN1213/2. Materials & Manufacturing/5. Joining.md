Why use 'joining' processes
- Assembly of parts - engineering system are complex
- Individual parts are easier to manufacture
- Easier maintenance - products can be disassembled
- Allows use of different materials in one system
- Promotes mass production and reduces cost

Mechanical joining
- Bolt, nut, screw, pin, rivit, stitch, staple, shrink fit, forge fit, seam, snap-in fixing

Adhesive bonding
- Polymeric adhesive

Brazing/Soldering
- Filler with lower melting point than base metal

Welding
- Weld joint is similar to. same as the base metal
- Solid or liquid state
- With or without filler

Solid satte welding:
- Heat and pressure applied or pressure alone applied below melting temperature of the material
- Filler material not usually used
- Requires clean mating faces to allow atomic bonding to occur
- Uses only heat to form weld
- Carried out above melting point of materials
- Possible with and without filler material
- Numerous processes available

Welding challenges: liquid state welding
- Composition changes
- Cavities, inclusions/contamination in the weld
- Additional cost of multi-run welds and joint preparation
- Residual stresses
- Most failures start at the HAZ
	- Grain growth, phase transformation
	- Sharpness of variation of HAZ may be reduced by preheating and/or shaping of weld faces

Forge welding
- Deformation in hot working condition
- Oxide and contaminants are squeezed out 
- Develop inter-atomic bonding
- High localised pressure applied in a vacuum
- No external heat applied
- Large amount of cold work, and some plastic deformation can occur
- Used for small parts
- Works well with ductile metals
- Can cause problems for equipment in space

Diffusion Welding
- Requires flat and highly polished surfaces
- Carried out in an inert atmosphere
- Surfaces held together under pressure and heated to around half their melting point
- Limited deformation occurs
- High bond strength achieved
- Slow process - 15 minutes to several days
- Good for Ti-based super-alloys often used in aircraft components

Friction welding
- Rotational friction and pressure creates temperature close to melting point
- Narrow heat affected zone
- Weld strength is almost the same as the base material - welded throughout
- Typically used for drive shafts

Ultrasonic welding
- Ultrasonic vibration and pressure applied
- Temperature rises to 1/3 to 1/2 of melting point
- Restricted to thin, small, delicate parts 
- Also used for plastics

High frequency welding
- Induction coil used to induce eddy currents in part
- Creates localized heat
- Pressure applied to forge the joint
- High current form preferentially along edges - heating localised at joint faces
- Small HAZ
- Highly conductive material can be easily joined

Explosive welding
- High temperature and pressure produced by explosion
- Con produce a bond not easily achieved by other means
- Large areas bonded very quickly
- Often used to weld metals with different melting points

Liquid state welding
- Uses heat to form weld
- Carried out above melting point of materials
- Possible with and without filler material
- Numerous processes available

Oxy-fuel Gas welding
- Heat generated by burning Acetylene and oxygen
- Usually undertaken with filler
- Oxide contamination leads to poor weld quality
- Heat is not concentrated 
- Process largely outdated

Arc Welding
- Electric arc provides heat around 5000C
- Gas required to stabilise the arc and provide protective environment
- Gas generated by melting flux or supplied through the nozzle

Submerged arc welding
- Uses granular flux and reel fed filler wire
- High speed processing and deep weld penetration
- Highly automated, but reduced flexibility

Gas metal arc welding
- Consumable electrode wire in reel form
- Shielding gas fed through nozzle
- Relatively easy to automate
- No/very little slag to remove
- Also known as MIG (Metal Inert Gas) or MAG (Metal Active Gas)

Gas tungsten arc
- Non consumable electrode supplies a constant arc
- Separate filler rof required
- Inert gas supplied as shield
- High weld quality
- Skilled operation required
- Slow processing speed
- High cost

Plasma arc welding / cutting processes
- High gas temperature due to lionisation
- Arc temperature : up to 20000C 
- Non-consumable electrode
- High energy concentration: localised heating, fast, deeper penetration and narrow HAS
- Often used as a cutting processes

Resistance:
- Contact resistance: between electrode and workpiece
- Weld surface resistance: between workpiece surfaces
Pressure:
- Reduced contact resistance
- Increased after solidification for forging

Projection welding
- Projections pre-pressed into components
- Multiple simultaneous welds possible
- Lower electrical contact resistance for same force
- Any shape of projection possible
- Precise location of welds
- Less wear on electrodes

Exothermic "Thermit" welding
- Heat produced by exothermic reaction such as aluminium and iron oxide
- Typical uses: - Welding rails/large casting
- Cutting scrap metal
